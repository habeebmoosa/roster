# Common research biases and pitfalls

A reference list of analytical traps to actively check for, not just avoid by accident.

## Correlation vs. causation

Two things moving together does not mean one causes the other — both could be driven by a third
factor, or the relationship could be coincidental. ("Ice cream sales and drowning rates both rise
in summer" — heat drives both; ice cream doesn't cause drowning.) Never state a causal claim
unless the evidence actually supports a causal mechanism, not just a correlation.

## Survivorship bias

Drawing conclusions only from the cases that "survived" or succeeded, while ignoring the failed
cases that never got studied or reported on. ("Successful startups all did X" ignores the failed
startups that also did X.) Always ask what the failure population looked like before crediting a
success factor.

## Confirmation bias

Favoring sources and findings that support a conclusion already suspected, while discounting
contradicting evidence. The fix is procedural: actively look for the strongest source that
disagrees with the working conclusion before finalizing it.

## Cherry-picking

Presenting the data points that support a claim while omitting ones that don't, without
disclosing the omission. If a finding excludes contradicting data, say so and explain why.

## Small-sample overgeneralization

Treating a small or non-representative sample as if it generalizes to the whole population.
Always note sample size and how the sample was selected alongside any claim built on it.

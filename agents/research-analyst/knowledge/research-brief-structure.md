# Research brief structure

The standard shape a research brief or findings summary should take, so a reader can scan it and
know where to find what they need.

## Sections

1. **Executive summary** — 2-4 sentences stating the core finding and its implication. A reader
   who only reads this section should still walk away with the answer, not just the topic.
2. **Methodology** — what sources were used, what was searched for, and what was explicitly out
   of scope. This is what lets a reader judge how much to trust the findings.
3. **Key findings** — the substantive content, organized by theme or question, not by source.
   Each finding should say what it's based on, not just assert it.
4. **Limitations and caveats** — what wasn't covered, what's uncertain, and where sources
   disagreed. Omitting this section is the most common way a brief overstates its own certainty.
5. **Sources** — every source actually cited in the findings, not a generic reading list.

## Primary vs. secondary research

- **Primary research**: original data collection — surveys you ran, interviews you conducted,
  direct experiments. Highest confidence, but slow and narrow in scope.
- **Secondary research**: synthesizing existing sources — published studies, reports, articles.
  Faster and broader, but only as reliable as the sources it draws on.

Most research briefs are secondary research. Say so explicitly rather than implying primary-level
rigor.

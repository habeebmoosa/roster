# Playbook: budget-vs-actual variance analysis

How to explain why actual results differed from budget — the point is the driver, not just the
number.

## 1. Separate volume variance from price/rate variance

Distinguish "we spent more because we did more of something" (volume variance — e.g. more
customer support tickets handled) from "we spent more per unit" (price/rate variance — e.g. the
same number of tickets but a higher cost per ticket). Blending these into one number hides which
one actually happened.

## 2. Distinguish one-time from recurring drivers

A variance caused by a one-time event (a single large refund, a one-off contractor invoice) has
different implications than one caused by a recurring shift (a permanently higher unit cost, a
sustained change in usage). State which kind of driver caused the variance — this determines
whether the variance should change the forecast going forward.

## 3. Don't just report the number — explain the driver

"Marketing spend was 15% under budget" is not an analysis, it's an observation. The analysis is
naming why: a campaign was delayed, a vendor contract came in cheaper than budgeted, or headcount
hiring was slower than planned. Always attach a driver to a variance, even a partial or
provisional one, rather than leaving the number to speak for itself.

## 4. Check whether a favorable variance actually masks a problem

A favorable (better-than-budget) variance is not automatically good news. Underspending on
marketing could mean hiring for the growth team is behind schedule, which will show up as a
revenue shortfall later. Always ask what a favorable variance implies about a _different_ line
item before calling it good news.

# Forecasting and assumptions

Reference for how to think about forecasts and the assumptions underneath them.

## A forecast is only as good as its stated assumptions

Every forecast number is a function of assumptions about growth rate, churn, pricing, and cost
inflation. Presenting a forecast number without the assumptions behind it invites false
confidence — always show what the number depends on, not just the number.

## Base, best, and worst case

Build a forecast as at least three scenarios: base case (the most likely set of assumptions),
best case (assumptions skew favorable but still plausible), and worst case (assumptions skew
unfavorable but still plausible) — not just a single point estimate. A single number implies more
certainty than any forecast actually has.

## Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis means flexing one assumption at a time (holding all others constant) to see
how much the output changes — this identifies which assumptions the forecast is most sensitive
to, so effort goes into validating those specifically rather than treating every input as equally
important.

## Common assumption categories to make explicit

- **Growth rate** — new customer or revenue growth rate, and whether it's assumed constant or
  changing over the forecast period.
- **Churn** — the rate existing customers are assumed to leave; small changes here compound
  significantly over a multi-year forecast.
- **Pricing** — whether prices are assumed flat or changing, and by how much.
- **Cost inflation** — whether unit costs (salaries, infrastructure, COGS) are assumed to rise,
  and at what rate.
